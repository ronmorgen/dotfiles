# Concept — explain what something is and why it matters

**Use when:** A reader needs foundational understanding of an idea, mechanism, or abstraction before they can act — the "what" and "why," not the "how."
**Don't use when:** The reader needs to *do* something (use a how-to or tutorial for steps) or *look up* fields/commands/params (use a reference). A concept has no procedures and no lookup tables of settings.
**Audience & voice:** Readers new to the idea or arriving mid-journey from a how-to prerequisite or related link. Conversational, clear, jargon-light; present tense, second person. Understanding-oriented — never instructional.

## Skeleton

```
# {Concept name}

{Summary paragraph: introduce the concept, explain its relevance/importance, and
state the scope of what this article covers. Typical opener: "This article explains
the basics of {concept} and how it works in {tool/context}."}

{Definition paragraph (usually no explicit "Definition" heading — it precedes
everything else). Define the concept so you and the reader share the language used
throughout. Typical wordings: "{X} is...", "{X} represents...", "{X} solves the
challenge of...", "By using {X}, {audience} gain...", "To use {X}, you create {Y}".}

{Add a visual aid to complement the explanation.}
(Optional) Image/Figure: {Title concisely explaining the image.}

## (Optional) Background
{Context, prehistory, or background. Where the idea originated, industry context,
relevant trends or regulations, why it was designed this way, alternatives.}

## Use cases
{How the reader benefits from the concept. Good place for a problem→guide→outcome
story: who the reader is, the problem they face, how the concept resolves it.}

## (Optional) Comparison of {thing being compared}
Table: {Title concisely explaining the comparison.}
| What | Why needed |
| ---- | ---------- |
| {type 1} | {reason to use it} |
| {type 2} | {reason to use it} |

## (Optional) Related resources
{Links for going deeper, grouped (How-to guides, Linked concepts, External
resources), 3-5 links per group max.}
```

## Filling tips

- **Summary / intro:** Use the inverted pyramid — lead with a high-level overview that sets relevance and scope, then narrow. State what's covered and what the reader will gain. Optional; you may jump straight to the definition.
- **Definition:** Give a shared, glossary-style definition and fix the scope: its boundaries, depth, and (usefully) what's *out* of scope. Disassociate from same-named concepts elsewhere. Connect it to concepts the reader already knows; analogies (water-in-pipes for electricity) bridge gaps if your audience will get them.
- **Visual aid:** One diagram is usually enough — place it near the top, under the description, to orient readers. Use diagrams to simplify relationships, processes, or hierarchies; add numbered elements with a legend for processes. Don't over-illustrate if text alone is clear.
- **Background:** Optional. Include only if history/context aids understanding — origins, legacy decisions, industry or technology trends. Skip if it doesn't deepen comprehension.
- **Use cases:** Apply storytelling (StoryBrand): make the reader the protagonist with a relatable problem, position the concept as the guide, show the transformed outcome. Balance engagement against clarity — don't let the story bury the concept.
- **Comparison:** Optional, for concepts with multiple types, versions, or a predecessor. Answer "how does this differ?" and "why choose this option?" in a compact what/why table.
- **Related resources:** Group links by kind and cap each group at 3-5 to avoid a wall of links. Prefer descriptive section names like "Related concepts" or "Additional information."
- **Overall:** Dedicate the doc to a *single* concept — split off and link if a second concept creeps in. Avoid instructional steps and implementation details. Address the 5W+1H early; chunk complex ideas under headings.
